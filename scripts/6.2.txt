# 6.2 The design space: seven routes to molecular AI

If you had to bet five years of your working life on one way of getting molecules to do the work of AI, which way would you choose?

You cannot answer that until you can see all the options side by side. So this lesson lays out seven routes. For each one I will tell you three things: what has been shown, what holds it back, and where a tenfold improvement could come from. Some of these are crowded. Some are nearly empty. One of them is not a route at all, but the road surface under the other six.

Quick recall. What is the dilution law? And if throughput is not the bottleneck for molecular computing, what is?

The dilution law: total concentration is capped, so each species you add leaves less for the others, and every reaction slows. And the bottleneck is organisation: addressing, precision, depth, and getting data in and out.

Route one: train in software, run in chemistry. You train a small network on an ordinary computer, then translate its weights into concentrations and build it. Almost every DNA neural network before twenty twenty-five was made this way. Four neurons in twenty eleven. Nine classes of hundred-bit patterns in twenty eighteen. Enzymatic networks in twenty twenty-two. Classifiers that read gene expression for diagnosis. What holds it back is the gap between the model and the tube. Your simulation says one thing, and leak, crosstalk and uncertain rate constants say another. And every design is a one-off. The tenfold levers are better predictors of what strands really do to each other, which is what BINND is, better compilers, and designs whose answers do not depend on reaction rates at all, like the rate-independent networks of Vasić and Soloveichik. This route is the workhorse. It is also where a molecular computer has its clearest practical use: making a decision inside a sample, where no chip can go.

Route two: put the tube in the training loop. This is physics-aware training, from phase two, applied to chemistry. The forward pass happens in the real tube. You read out the result. A digital model of the chemistry works out how to adjust the concentrations. A robot makes the adjustment, and you go round again. The tube's own quirks get trained around instead of modelled away. To my knowledge, nobody has yet done this at any scale for a DNA circuit. What it needs is fast read-out, automation, a differentiable model of the reactions, and circuits that can be run more than once. Each of those now exists somewhere. A mass-action model is a set of differential equations, and you can differentiate through those. Heat recharging gives you at least sixteen runs. This route is open, and it suits someone who thinks in kinetics and automation.

Route three: learning rules in the chemistry itself. No computer in the loop at all. The molecules change themselves in response to examples. The first real demonstration came in twenty twenty-five, from Kevin Cherry and Lulu Qian: a network that is shown training patterns as molecules and writes them into its own concentrations. That learning is additive. It memorises. It does not yet correct its own mistakes. What holds this route back is that error-driven learning needs subtraction, a sign, and many repeated rounds, and each of those is hard in chemistry. The levers are reuse, and enzymes, which can keep a system running instead of letting it wind down. Of the seven, this is the most novel as science, and the furthest from use.

[B] Those three all try to make chemistry behave like a neural network. Is there a route that doesn't?

Yes, and it starts from the other end. Routes four, five and six ask what molecules already do well, and then look for the AI workload that matches.

Route four: computing centred on storage. The data is in DNA anyway. So bring the computation to it. Three things have been shown. Similarity search across one point six million images, in twenty twenty-one. Programs run in parallel on every stored register, in twenty twenty-three. And from NC State in twenty twenty-four, a material that holds files, lets you read them without using them up, and supports simple enzymatic computation. What holds it back: writing is expensive, reading is slow, the set of operations is small, and binding gets less specific as the archive grows. The levers: encodings learned by a neural network, so that similar things bind. Binding predictors like BINND, so that you can design which things stick. And cheaper writing. The AI role is easy to state. Modern models lean constantly on retrieval: find me the stored items closest to this one. That operation is limited by memory, it tolerates delay, and it is what hybridisation does. This is the route where the molecular step is already the natural one.

Route five: evolution and selection as the learning algorithm. Chemists have done this for decades and never called it machine learning. You make a million million variants. You apply a selection: only the ones that bind, or catalyse, survive. You amplify the survivors with a few mutations. You repeat. That is an optimiser. The population is the batch, selection is the loss function, amplification with mutation is the update. It needs only forward evaluations, it is perfectly parallel, and it needs tens of rounds where gradient descent needs a million. What holds it back is that the thing being optimised is the molecule itself. To make it learn something else, you would have to write a model's parameters into sequence, and build a selection that rewards computing the right answer. Nobody has a general way to do that. It is the least explored route on this list, and it is the one that uses DNA's most distinctive trick, which is that it can be copied with variation.

Route six: let the answer be the equilibrium. Instead of driving a system through a sequence of steps, shape its energy landscape so that the state it settles into is the answer. In twenty twenty-four, a set of nine hundred and seventeen DNA tiles classified images by which of three structures nucleated first. It took a hundred and fifty hours. In twenty twenty-six, the Maynooth computer ran ten programs by relaxing to equilibrium, could be reused dozens of times, and finished small instances in under a minute. What holds this route back is knowing how to design landscapes for large problems, and how settling time grows with size. The lever is theory that already exists. Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines are energy landscapes. Sampling and optimisation are things AI needs. This is the dark horse.

Route seven is the road surface: space. Tethering gates side by side on a scaffold. Compartments, in droplets or in porous particles. Enzymes that supply power. Living cells. None of these is a way of doing AI. Each of them loosens a limit for the other six. Put two gates next to each other and the dilution law stops applying, because local concentration no longer depends on what else is in the tube. Give things positions and you can reuse the same sequences in different places, which is how a chip gets billions of wires. Make a million droplets and you have a million small independent computers. If the earlier routes are algorithms, route seven is the hardware.

[B] You said some architectures favour the opposite of what silicon does. What does that mean in practice?

It means this, and I will state it as a hypothesis, because that is all it is. For molecules, depth is expensive and width is nearly free. For chips it is the other way about. So the neural networks everyone uses are deep and modestly wide, because that is what chips reward. The natural molecular network would be extremely wide and very shallow. Two questions stand in the way. The first belongs to machine learning: can a very wide, shallow model do what a deep one does? There are reasons to think width can stand in for some depth, and there is no settled answer. The second belongs to you: a wide layer needs a wide address space, and only the storage row of the scorecard has one. So even this idea leads back to routes four and seven.

One more thing before we stop. In every realistic version, the machine is a hybrid. An electronic controller handles sequencing, arithmetic and decisions. A molecular co-processor handles one step. So here is the test to apply to any proposal, your own included. Which step is the molecular step? And in the all-electronic version of the system, is that step the bottleneck? If it is not, the molecular version has nothing to win.

[B] So which would you bet on?

Here is a judgment, and treat it as one. For usefulness soon: route four, on top of route seven. For a first-of-its-kind demonstration: route two, the tube in the loop, because every ingredient now exists and nobody has put them together. For the long game: route six. And route five is the one I would keep reading about, because it is so empty.

Three sentences. Three routes make chemistry imitate a neural network: train in software, train with the tube in the loop, or learn in the chemistry. Three start from what molecules do well: retrieval from storage, selection across huge populations, and settling to equilibrium. Under all of them lies space, which is what loosens the limits on wiring and speed.

[B] Questions. One: name the seven routes. Two: for route four, say which step is the molecular step, and why it is a bottleneck in the electronic version. Three: what are the two open questions behind the idea of very wide, very shallow molecular networks?
